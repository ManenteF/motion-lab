# Ideation and Directions

Use when the user gives a motion reference, still reference, rough concept, key visual, or only a loose idea. **Do not implement immediately.** First propose directions — briefly — then pick or infer and build.

(In MATCH REFERENCE mode the reference already sets the direction; skip directions and go to the reference reading and adaptation plan. You may still offer alternatives for the *adaptation* when a choice materially changes the result.)

## What a direction contains

Each of the 2–3 directions is a few lines, covering:

- **Central motion idea** — the one mechanism the piece is built on.
- **What moves** and **what stays still**.
- **Transition logic** — how it gets from one state to the next and why.
- **Rhythm** — fast/slow/held, density, where the peaks are.
- **How the assets are used** — which supplied elements, how they are separated, treated or reconstructed.
- **How the reference influences it** (if any) — what is taken, what is not.
- **Expected visual character** — what it will feel and look like.
- **Technical feasibility** — achievable / approximable / partial, and the tool it implies.

## Directions must be visually distinguishable

Directions that differ only in adjectives are not directions.

**BAD**
1. Smooth animation
2. Dynamic animation
3. Cinematic animation

**GOOD**
1. The logo geometry breaks into radial segments and reconstructs on impact.
2. The camera pushes through the negative space of the logo into the next scene.
3. The logo stays static while surrounding line-work builds its silhouette and then collapses into the mark.

Test: could someone storyboard each direction differently *without reading the adjectives*? If not, rewrite.

## How to generate strong directions

Work from the material, not from effect names:

- **Geometry of the asset** — what shapes, negative spaces, edges, counters, grids or symmetries does it contain? Motion that is derived from this geometry feels specific.
- **The brand's physical world** — material, weight, speed, noise level, tone of voice.
- **The reference's mechanism** — what *produces* the reference's energy (cuts? scale contrast? mask logic? camera? overlap?), and how could that mechanism be applied to a different subject.
- **Contrasting axes** — vary one structural axis per direction (e.g. object-driven vs. camera-driven vs. layout-driven; continuous vs. cut-based; build vs. break vs. reveal).
- **Constraints as ideas** — a low-resolution asset, a single color, a fixed lockup can each suggest a direction.

## Format and length

- Concise: roughly 3–6 lines each. No tables of numbers, no frame sheets at this stage.
- Lead with the visual idea in one sentence, then the specifics.
- State a recommendation: which direction you would build and why, in one line.
- Mention a feasibility caveat inline when it matters ("needs the mark separated into layers — approximable from the supplied PNG").

## Choosing

- If the brief clearly points to one, proceed and say which you took.
- If two are equally strong and cheap to build, build the recommended one and note the other is available.
- If the choice is a real fork that would waste substantial effort (e.g. 3D vs. flat, different delivery formats), ask once, briefly.
- Combine directions only when they genuinely merge into one idea, not to avoid choosing.

## After choosing

Keep pre-implementation notes short — the direction, what moves, rough timing, the chosen tool. Then build the first version, review the real frames, and refine. Do not write long plans before there is something to look at.
